Chronology of the reported apparitions of Our Lady of Tears in Campinas, Brazil, and of what came before and after them (1908–2023)

An open, source-referenced chronology of the reported apparitions of Our Lady of Tears (Nossa Senhora das Lágrimas) to Sister Amália de Jesus Flagelado in Campinas, Brazil, and of the Chaplet of Tears devotion that grew from them.

How far the case went

Each rung is a separate act by a separate authority, about a separate object. The page records what each one did and when, citing the act; it does not add them up, and there is no overall verdict to report. For Campinas the finding is an absence: no judgment by any Church authority on the apparitions themselves has been located, in devotional, institutional or academic sources. The one primary Church document in this dataset — the Archdiocese of Campinas decree of 16 June 2023 (Livro XXXII dos Atos de Governo, Prot. 106) — creates a commission to study Sister Amália's possible odor of sanctity with a view to the possible opening of a beatification cause. It concerns one person's cause, says nothing whatever about the reported apparitions, and is therefore not a rung on this ladder.

Step by step, with the documents

Local inquiry in Campinas 1928–1930

·No record found that this step took place

Diocese of Campinas under Bishop Francisco de Campos Barreto

The 1917 Code of Canon Law provided for a diocesan tribunal to examine reported miracles, and required prudence, silence, and verification by suitable persons appointed by the ecclesiastical authority before anything was published. The bishop did enforce the silence — he closed access to Sister Amália and turned journalists away — but no canonically constituted commission, tribunal or appointed investigator appears in any source consulted. The investigations the newspapers called "inquéritos" were run by the press, by physicians and by clergy of several denominations, not by the diocese. Santos reads Barreto's own conduct as the reverse of the canonical path: rather than notify Rome and keep silent, he became a public promoter of the events.

Searched: the recitals of the 2023 archdiocesan decree, the Archdiocese of Campinas website, the congregation's own published history (which, as consulted on 5 August 2026, does not mention Sister Amália or the apparitions at all), the Centro de Memória da Unicamp account of the case, and Santos's 2025 study, which works from the contemporary São Paulo press. None names a diocesan body charged with judging the phenomena. Any such record would sit in the acts of government held at the archdiocesan archive in Campinas (Cúria Metropolitana), which was not consulted for this dataset.

The bishop's judgment on the apparitions reported 8 March 1931

?A ruling is reported; no document located

Dom Francisco de Campos Barreto, bishop of Campinas 1920–1941

Devotional literature, followed by the Portuguese and English Wikipedia articles, states that on 8 March 1931 Barreto recognized the veracity of Sister Amália's stigmata and apparitions. No source consulted reproduces that act, quotes it, or gives an archival reference for it, and the 2023 archdiocesan decree does not mention it. What is documented is different in kind: in November 1928 Barreto told the São Paulo press that the case was a true stigmatization and "miraculous". An opinion given to a newspaper — which Santos reads as out of step with the canonical norms then in force — is not a judgment on an apparition. Since this rung was written a second study by the same historian (Santos 2019) has been located. It dates Barreto's imprimatur for the chaplet and the medal to 8 March 1932 — the same day and month as the reported act, one year later — and records nothing at all for 8 March 1931. It does not describe any act of his recognising the apparitions, on that date or on any other.

No text, protocol number or archival reference for an 8 March 1931 act was located in any source consulted, devotional, institutional or academic; the sources that carry the claim cite no document and repeat one another. It would be found, if it exists, among the acts of government of the then Diocese of Campinas, in the archdiocesan archive at the Cúria Metropolitana de Campinas, which was not consulted here. That remains true after Santos (2019): he supplies a date for the act on the DEVOTION and none for an act on the APPARITIONS, and reproduces neither. Three devotional videos recorded in the 2020s by Raphael Tonon restate an episcopal approval and add that a report by a Vatican-appointed investigator on the case was found in the Vatican Apostolic Archive; no protocol number, date or text was given for it, and it is treated here as a lead for that archive rather than as evidence.

Permission to print the writings — not a verdict on the apparitions 1933

◆Church act on a related matter — not a ruling on the apparition

Dom Francisco de Campos Barreto

Barreto granted the imprimatur for Licções de Jesus Victima, the volume of messages attributed to "Jesus Manietado" published by the Institute in Campinas in 1933, and wrote its preface. This is the only episcopal act in the case for which a scholarly source supplies a bibliographic citation and a quotation. Its object is a book. An imprimatur is a permission to print, granted after censorship for faith and morals; it settles nothing about whether the reported apparitions were supernatural, which is why it stands here as an act on a related matter and not as a verdict on the rung before it.

Further acts on the devotion, in Brazil and abroad 8 March 1932; abroad, March–July 1935; a declaration of 20 February 1934 reported only

◆Church act on a related matter — not a ruling on the apparition

Bishop Barreto; unnamed bishops in the United States, Germany and Hungary

Devotional literature also reports an imprimatur of 8 March 1931 covering the chaplet prayers, an episcopal declaration of 20 February 1934 in which Barreto reinforced the importance of the devotion to Our Lady of Tears, and further imprimaturs for the chaplet granted by bishops abroad from 1935. Every one of these concerns the devotion and its printed texts rather than the apparitions, so even if all were produced tomorrow they would leave the rung above unanswered. Santos (2019) now dates and attributes several of these: he gives 8 March 1932, not 1931, for the Brazilian imprimatur on the chaplet and the medal, and names the approvals abroad individually — Detroit under Bishop Michael James Gallagher and Munich-Freising under vicar general Ferdinand Buchwieser, both 22 March 1935; a nihil obstat numbered 924/1935 from the diocesan censor at Sopron, 25 May 1935; and Győr under Bishop Stephanus Breyer, 13 July 1935. He records no act of 20 February 1934.

None of these texts has been produced by any source consulted. What changed with Santos (2019) is that they are no longer bare assertions: an academic footnote supplies dates, granting authorities, sees and — for Sopron — the only protocol number attaching to any act in this case that any source gives. The acts themselves are still unseen, so what is recorded here is an attested act rather than a reproduced one; the evidence behind this rung is nevertheless materially stronger than behind the rung above it, and the reported declaration of 20 February 1934 remains unattested by anything. The Brazilian act would be in the archdiocesan archive at the Cúria Metropolitana de Campinas, whose Livro do Tombo a devotional researcher states he has consulted; the foreign ones in the chanceries of Detroit, Munich and Freising, Sopron and Győr.

A ruling by the Holy See 1929 onwards

·No record found that this step took place

Roman Curia; apostolic nunciature to Brazil

Rome was not absent from the case. In May 1929 the apostolic nuncio to Brazil, Benedetto Aloisi Masella, spent a week in Campinas following the celebrations and the reported manifestations at the Institute, with the São Paulo press covering each step and describing him as pleased with what he saw. But a visit is not an act: no decree, rescript, response or report of the Holy See or of any Roman dicastery on the Campinas apparitions has been located, and no source consulted refers to one. Santos (2019) states flatly that the messages and the devotion have not been approved by the Holy See. Against that, the three devotional videos added to this dataset do assert Roman action: that Pius XI appointed an investigator, that a canonical process was opened in 1935 with the Benedictine Dom Lourenço Zeller as apostolic visitor, that Zeller reported favourably, and that a decree of the Holy Office then ordered the images withdrawn and public cult suspended. That is an assertion of a restrictive Roman act, not a favourable one, and it is undocumented in every particular.

This rung is "no ruling found", not "the case never went to Rome", and the distinction is the point. Recording it as "did not reach" would require positive evidence that the case was never referred, and there is none: the nuncio's 1929 visit shows Roman attention, while the claim that Barreto skipped the expected notification to the Holy See rests on a contemporary priest's criticism in the press and on Santos's reading of it, not on a document. Searched: every source in this dataset, the Archdiocese of Campinas website, Catholic media coverage of the 2023 commission, and open web searches for any Holy See or Doctrine of the Faith act on the Campinas case. The Acta Apostolicae Sedis of the 1930s and the nunciature's own files were not consulted, and either could still hold something. One qualification has been added since: three devotional videos now cited in this dataset DO refer to Roman acts — an appointment by Pius XI, a 1935 apostolic visitation and a decree of the Holy Office — without a date, a protocol number or a text for any of them, and no institutional or academic source consulted mentions any of the three. The person named is real and partly checks out: Dom Lourenço Zeller OSB (Riedlingen 1873 – Belém 1945) was a German-born Benedictine in Brazil, appointed titular bishop of Dorylæum by Pius XI on 7 January 1939 and buried at the monastery in Rio de Janeiro. His role in this case is what is unattested — and the videos misdate his episcopal appointment to 1945 and call him abbot of Rio, which he never was. These remain leads for the Vatican Apostolic Archive and the Campinas curia.

About

Work-in-progress dataset compiled from public sources (see references). The apparitions are recorded as REPORTED events: the dataset states who reported what and when, and what Church authorities have ruled and when — it does not assert the supernatural claims as fact. The scope now runs wider than the reported events themselves, carrying the founding of the congregation and the state of the diocese before them and the congregation's growth, the spread of the devotion, the visionary's later life and the 2023 commission after them. Reported cures and wonders are recorded only as ACCOUNTS — that something was reported, by whom, and when first attested — never as events that happened, and they carry no rung on the approval ladder. Contested claims are attributed to their authors; dates not yet verified against a primary source are flagged with a ? mark. Much of the devotional literature on this subject is derivative and cites no documents, and the recent video commentary comes from a single speaker who also sits on the archdiocesan study commission; the provenance notes on each reference say so. Corrections against primary sources are welcome.

What it is
A series of reported apparitions of Jesus (8 November 1929, as "Jesus Manietado", Jesus Bound) and of the Virgin Mary presenting herself as "Nossa Senhora das Lágrimas" (Our Lady of Tears, 8 March and 8 April 1930), to Sister Amália de Jesus Flagelado in the chapel of the Missionárias de Jesus Crucificado mother house in Campinas, São Paulo, Brazil. The Chaplet (Coroa/Terço) of Tears devotion traces its origin to these reports.[7] [9] [6]
Reported visionary
Sister Amália de Jesus Flagelado, born Amália Aguirre (Riós, Ourense, Galicia, Spain, 22 July 1901 – Taubaté, Brazil, 18 April 1977), a member of the first group of the Instituto das Missionárias de Jesus Crucificado.[1] [6] [10]
Church ruling on the apparitions
No judgment by Church authority on the apparitions themselves could be located. What IS dated is narrower and concerns the devotion: an academic source (Santos 2019) records an imprimatur signed by Bishop Francisco de Campos Barreto on 8 March 1932 authorising the chaplet and the medal within the diocese, and names four approvals abroad in 1935, one of them carrying a protocol number. Devotional literature instead reports an episcopal recognition of the apparitions and an imprimatur on 8 March 1931, and a declaration of 20 February 1934; no copy or archival citation of either was found, and neither academic study of the case records them. The one located primary document — the archdiocesan decree of 16 June 2023 — makes no pronouncement on the apparitions. Santos states that the messages and the devotion have still not been approved by the Holy See.[14] [3] [1] [11] [7]
Approval of the chaplet vs. approval of the apparitions
These are distinct acts. An imprimatur or authorization to publish prayers permits a devotion; it is not a declaration that the apparitions were supernatural. Three such permissions are dated by academic sources and all three have a printed text or an object as their object: Barreto's imprimatur of 8 March 1932 for the Chaplet and the Medal of Tears, his imprimatur and preface for the 1933 volume “Licções de Jesus Victima”, and his 1934 preface to “Glórias e Poder de Nossa Senhora das Lágrimas”; to these the 1935 approvals by bishops in the United States, Germany and Hungary add permissions to print the prayers in other jurisdictions. This dataset records no located Church declaration of supernaturality for the Campinas apparitions.[14] [3] [11] [7]
Status of the visionary's cause
On 16 June 2023 Archbishop João Inácio Müller of Campinas decreed the creation of a Study Commission "Ir. Amália de Jesus Flagelado" to investigate her possible "odor of sanctity" with a view to the POSSIBLE opening of a beatification cause. As of that decree, no cause had been opened and no judgment on her sanctity or on the apparitions had been made.[1] [6]

Campinas, Brazil Reported stigmata become public; press controversy — Sister Amália's reported stigmata, said to have begun earlier in 1928, become publicly known and draw heavy press attention. 1929 1929 ? Campinas, Brazil The Institute's own periodical publishes the account of the cure the devotion's origin story turns on — The devotional tradition traces the whole devotion to a reported cure: a man came to the convent door because his wife had been given up by her doctors, and a sister — unnamed in the original telling — offered her own life for the mother's. Raphael Tonon states that he holds an original of “A Missionária”, the Institute's own periodical, in which Bishop Barreto authorised the sisters to publish the account, with the people involved reduced to initials; he reads from it and dates the episode to 1929, and adds, from later family testimony, that the sick woman was the wife of a brother of Madre Villac. What is recorded here is that the account was PUBLISHED and by whom — not that a cure occurred. 1941 1941-08-22 Campinas, Brazil Death of Bishop Francisco de Campos Barreto — Francisco de Campos Barreto, second diocesan bishop of Campinas (installed 14 November 1920) and founder of the Instituto das Missionárias de Jesus Crucificado, dies in office.[12] [1]
1941 1941-12-31 ? Campinas, Brazil After Barreto's death the devotion is withdrawn from public view — Santos (2019) records that following the death of Bishop Barreto in 1941 the devotion to Our Lady of Tears was moved backstage in Campinas: the chapel where the apparition was reported was closed to public access and reserved to the sisters, the devotional image of the Virgin of Tears was taken down from the cathedral altar and moved to the convent chapel, and the book “Glórias e Poder de Nossa Senhora das Lágrimas” disappeared from the holdings of Catholic libraries. He attributes the silence to the death of the case's chief promoter and to a return of prudence, and names no act of any authority ordering it.[14]Santos places these developments after Barreto's death of 22 August 1941 without giving them dates; the event is filed at the end of 1941 so that it follows his death, and the date is flagged unverified for that reason. Raphael Tonon gives a different and much stronger account of the same withdrawal: that a canonical process was opened in 1935, that the Holy See appointed the Benedictine Dom Lourenço Zeller apostolic visitor, that Zeller reported favourably to the Holy Office, and that a Holy Office decree then ordered the images withdrawn and public cult suspended while private recitation of the chaplet remained free. No text, date or protocol number was produced for that decree, no source consulted outside the three videos refers to it, and Zeller's own biography contradicts part of the account (see the disambiguation note). Disambiguation & nuance

Reported private revelations are contested terrain by nature: the primary phenomena are attested mainly by devotional literature that recycles itself, while the institutional record is thin. These distinctions keep the claims from flattening into each other.

Campinas (1930) ≠ Syracuse (1953)

"Our Lady of Tears" names more than one case. The Campinas events are reported apparitions to a religious sister (1929–1930). The Madonna delle Lacrime of Syracuse, Sicily, is a different case — a plaster image reported to have wept on 29 August–1 September 1953 — with its own, and much stronger, ecclesiastical record: the bishops of Sicily declared in December 1953 that the weeping was real, and Pius XII spoke of it in a radio message of 17 October 1954. Neither case's status transfers to the other.[13] [7]

Imprimatur on the prayers ≠ approval of the apparitions

The approval devotional literature reports for 1931 is an authorization to publish Sister Amália's writings and the chaplet prayers. An imprimatur is a permission to print, not a declaration that an apparition was supernatural. No declaration of supernaturality for the Campinas apparitions was located in any source consulted.[11] [7]

The 2023 commission ≠ an opened cause, and it is not about the apparitions

The 16 June 2023 decree creates a commission to study whether a beatification cause for Sister Amália may POSSIBLY be opened, examining her "odor of sanctity or not" ("odor de santidade ou menos"). It is not an opened cause, not a beatification, and it pronounces nothing about the apparitions. Some tertiary sources describe it as the opening of a canonical beatification process; the decree's own text is more limited.[1] [6]

The Institute's history ≠ the devotional narrative

The Missionárias de Jesus Crucificado's own published history (as consulted on 5 August 2026) does not mention Sister Amália or the apparitions; it centers on Dom Barreto and Maria Villac. The apparition narrative is transmitted by devotional websites and publishers, which largely repeat one another without citing documents. The two bodies of literature should not be read as one voice.[5] [8]

The chief populariser of the devotion sits on the commission studying it

Almost all recent public exposition of the Campinas case in Brazil comes from one man, Raphael Tonon, in lectures and podcasts from the 2020s. The 16 June 2023 archdiocesan decree names “Prof. Luiz Raphael Tonon” among the cooperators of the study commission on Sister Amália, alongside “Profª. Rita Elisa Sêda Pinto Müller”, the biographer, and “Pe. Rafael Capelato”, one of its coordinators, whom Tonon describes as having written a Gregorian thesis on Bishop Barreto. This matters in two directions. It means his account is better informed than an outsider's and that he has had access to material others have not — he says he has read the Livro do Tombo in the archdiocesan archive. It also means that agreement between the devotional literature, the popular videos and the people advising the archdiocese is not independent corroboration: it is a small circle of the same researchers. Nothing he says in the videos is an act of the archdiocese, and he says so himself, reporting that the archbishop told him to publish as a layman and that the Church would judge by results.[1] [20] [21] [22]

Why the devotion went quiet: two incompatible explanations

Everyone agrees that after the 1930s the devotion was withdrawn from public view in Campinas — the image off the cathedral altar, the chapel closed, the books out of the libraries. The explanations do not agree. The academic account (Santos 2019) attaches the silence to the death in 1941 of Bishop Barreto, the case's chief promoter, and to a return of prudence, and names no order from anyone. The devotional account attaches it to an act of authority six years earlier: a canonical process opened in 1935, an apostolic visitor sent by the Holy See, and a decree of the Holy Office restricting public cult while leaving private recitation free. The second version would mean Rome ruled on the case; no text, date or protocol number has ever been produced for it, and no institutional or academic source refers to it. This dataset records both and adopts neither, and the approval ladder continues to report the Holy See rung as “no ruling found” rather than as a ruling.[14] [20] [22]
