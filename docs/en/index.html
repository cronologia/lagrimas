How far the case went

Each rung is a separate act by a separate authority, about a separate object. The page records what each one did and when, citing the act; it does not add them up, and there is no overall verdict to report. For Campinas the finding is an absence: no judgment by any Church authority on the apparitions themselves has been located, in devotional, institutional or academic sources. The one primary Church document in this dataset — the Archdiocese of Campinas decree of 16 June 2023 (Livro XXXII dos Atos de Governo, Prot. 106) — creates a commission to study Sister Amália's possible odor of sanctity with a view to the possible opening of a beatification cause. It concerns one person's cause, says nothing whatever about the reported apparitions, and is therefore not a rung on this ladder.

About

Work-in-progress dataset compiled from public sources (see references). The apparitions are recorded as REPORTED events: the dataset states who reported what and when, and what Church authorities have ruled and when — it does not assert the supernatural claims as fact. Contested claims are attributed to their authors; dates not yet verified against a primary source are flagged with a ? mark. Much of the devotional literature on this subject is derivative and cites no documents; the provenance notes on each reference say so. Corrections against primary sources are welcome.

What it is
A series of reported apparitions of Jesus (8 November 1929, as "Jesus Manietado", Jesus Bound) and of the Virgin Mary presenting herself as "Nossa Senhora das Lágrimas" (Our Lady of Tears, 8 March and 8 April 1930), to Sister Amália de Jesus Flagelado in the chapel of the Missionárias de Jesus Crucificado mother house in Campinas, São Paulo, Brazil. The Chaplet (Coroa/Terço) of Tears devotion traces its origin to these reports.[7] [9] [6]
Reported visionary
Sister Amália de Jesus Flagelado, born Amália Aguirre (Riós, Ourense, Galicia, Spain, 22 July 1901 – Taubaté, Brazil, 18 April 1977), a member of the first group of the Instituto das Missionárias de Jesus Crucificado.[1] [6] [10]
Church ruling on the apparitions
No judgment by Church authority on the apparitions themselves could be located. Devotional literature states that Bishop Francisco de Campos Barreto of Campinas recognized the phenomena and granted an imprimatur for Sister Amália's writings and the chaplet prayers on 8 March 1931, but no copy or archival citation of that document was found, and the one located primary document — the archdiocesan decree of 16 June 2023 — makes no pronouncement on the apparitions. The academic study of the 1928–1929 controversy records no canonically constituted diocesan inquiry either; the Holy See's nuncio to Brazil spent a week in Campinas in May 1929, but no Roman act arising from that visit was located.[11] [7] [1] [3]
Approval of the chaplet vs. approval of the apparitions
These are distinct acts. An imprimatur or authorization to publish prayers permits a devotion; it is not a declaration that the apparitions were supernatural. The reported 1931 authorization concerns publication of the writings and prayers. One imprimatur by Bishop Barreto is documented with a bibliographic citation — the one he granted for the 1933 volume "Licções de Jesus Victima", whose preface he also wrote — and that act too is a permission to print a book. This dataset records no located Church declaration of supernaturality for the Campinas apparitions.[11] [7] [3]
Status of the visionary's cause
On 16 June 2023 Archbishop João Inácio Müller of Campinas decreed the creation of a Study Commission "Ir. Amália de Jesus Flagelado" to investigate her possible "odor of sanctity" with a view to the POSSIBLE opening of a beatification cause. As of that decree, no cause had been opened and no judgment on her sanctity or on the apparitions had been made.[1] [6]

Key figures

Amália Aguirre (Sister Amália de Jesus Flagelado)

1901–1977 · Spain / Brazil

Reported visionary and stigmatic; member of the first group of the Instituto das Missionárias de Jesus Crucificado (1928). Her reported stigmata (1928) and apparitions (1929–1930) are the origin of the Chaplet of Tears devotion. Since 16 June 2023 an archdiocesan study commission examines her possible "odor of sanctity" with a view to the possible opening of a beatification cause.[1] [6] [2]

Francisco de Campos Barreto

1877–1941 · Brazil

Second diocesan bishop of Campinas (appointed 30 July 1920, installed 14 November 1920, died in office 1941); previously bishop of Pelotas (1911–1920). Founder, with Maria Villac, of the Instituto das Missionárias de Jesus Crucificado (1928). Contemporary critics accused him of promoting the Amália case (the Unicamp account records Dr. Sousa Ribeiro's accusation); devotional literature credits him with recognizing the phenomena and authorizing the devotion's publications in 1931 — a document not located here.[12] [2] [5]

Maria Villac (Madre Maria do Calvário)

1894–? · Brazil

Foundress and first superior of the Instituto das Missionárias de Jesus Crucificado; led the association from which the congregation grew. Born in Campinas on 26 February 1894; a death date was not established from the sources consulted.[5] [8]

João Inácio Müller

Brazil

Archbishop Metropolitan of Campinas; signed the 16 June 2023 decree creating the Study Commission "Ir. Amália de Jesus Flagelado".[1] [6]

Related organizations

Missionárias de Jesus Crucificado (MJC)

Founded 1928 · Campinas, Brazil

The congregation Sister Amália belonged to; the apparitions are reported to have occurred in the chapel of its mother house in Campinas. Recognized of pontifical right by the Decretum Laudis of Pius XII (1952); today present in Brazil, Bolivia, Paraguay, Angola and Mozambique. Note: the congregation's own published history page, as consulted on 5 August 2026, recounts the founding by Dom Barreto and Maria Villac without mentioning Sister Amália or the apparitions — the apparition narrative is carried by devotional literature, not by the Institute's institutional account.[5] [1]

Archdiocese of Campinas

Campinas, Brazil

The Church authority territorially competent for the case. Its bishop Francisco de Campos Barreto founded the Institute and, per devotional literature, authorized the devotion's publications (1931, document not located); its archbishop João Inácio Müller created the 2023 study commission on Sister Amália. No ruling by it on the apparitions themselves was located.[1] [12]

Disambiguation & nuance

Reported private revelations are contested terrain by nature: the primary phenomena are attested mainly by devotional literature that recycles itself, while the institutional record is thin. These distinctions keep the claims from flattening into each other.

Campinas (1930) ≠ Syracuse (1953)

"Our Lady of Tears" names more than one case. The Campinas events are reported apparitions to a religious sister (1929–1930). The Madonna delle Lacrime of Syracuse, Sicily, is a different case — a plaster image reported to have wept on 29 August–1 September 1953 — with its own, and much stronger, ecclesiastical record: the bishops of Sicily declared in December 1953 that the weeping was real, and Pius XII spoke of it in a radio message of 17 October 1954. Neither case's status transfers to the other.[13] [7]

Imprimatur on the prayers ≠ approval of the apparitions

The approval devotional literature reports for 1931 is an authorization to publish Sister Amália's writings and the chaplet prayers. An imprimatur is a permission to print, not a declaration that an apparition was supernatural. No declaration of supernaturality for the Campinas apparitions was located in any source consulted.[11] [7]

The 2023 commission ≠ an opened cause, and it is not about the apparitions

The 16 June 2023 decree creates a commission to study whether a beatification cause for Sister Amália may POSSIBLY be opened, examining her "odor of sanctity or not" ("odor de santidade ou menos"). It is not an opened cause, not a beatification, and it pronounces nothing about the apparitions. Some tertiary sources describe it as the opening of a canonical beatification process; the decree's own text is more limited.[1] [6]

The Institute's history ≠ the devotional narrative

The Missionárias de Jesus Crucificado's own published history (as consulted on 5 August 2026) does not mention Sister Amália or the apparitions; it centers on Dom Barreto and Maria Villac. The apparition narrative is transmitted by devotional websites and publishers, which largely repeat one another without citing documents. The two bodies of literature should not be read as one voice.[5] [8]
